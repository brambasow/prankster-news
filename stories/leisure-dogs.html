Man's Best Friend, Backed by Science: What Research Says About the Health Benefits of Owning a Dog

Dog owners have long said their pets make life better.

11:45 PM EST November 14, 2026 · 4 min read · by Prankster News Staff

Dog owners have long said their pets make life better. A growing body of research suggests they may be right, and not just emotionally. Large studies covering millions of people link dog ownership to longer lives, healthier hearts, more exercise and even lower odds of dementia. Here's what the science shows, and where it's still unsettled.

Dog owners tend to live longer

The strongest evidence concerns survival. In 2019, researchers led by Dr. Caroline Kramer published a meta-analysis in the American Heart Association journal “Circulation: Cardiovascular Quality and Outcomes”. It pooled 10 studies covering more than 3.8 million people followed for an average of about 10 years. Dog ownership was associated with a 24% lower risk of death from any cause and a 31% lower risk of death from cardiovascular disease. Among people who had already had a coronary event, living with a dog was linked to a 65% lower risk of death.

One of the largest single studies came from Sweden, where dogs must be registered. Published in “Scientific Reports” in 2017, it followed more than 3.4 million Swedes aged 40 to 80 for up to 12 years. After controlling for age, sex, marital status and income, dog owners had about a 20% lower risk of death overall and a 23% lower risk of cardiovascular death.

A boost after a heart attack or stroke

A follow-up Swedish study, published in 2019, looked at about 182,000 heart attack patients and 155,000 stroke patients. Dog owners had a lower risk of death after either event. The difference was largest for people who lived alone: heart attack survivors living alone with a dog had a 33% lower risk of death, and stroke survivors living alone had a 27% lower risk.

More walking, more often

One likely reason is simple: dogs need walks. A 2019 study of households in Cheshire, England, found dog owners were about 14 times more likely to walk for recreation. They also had four times the odds of meeting the recommended 150 minutes of physical activity a week. Dog walking didn't replace other exercise; it added to it. Children in dog-owning homes also walked more and spent more time in unstructured active play.

A built-in bonding hormone

The bond between people and dogs shows up in brain chemistry. A 2015 study in Science by Japanese researchers found that when dogs gazed at their owners, the owners' levels of oxytocin, a hormone tied to bonding and trust, rose. The dogs' oxytocin rose too. In a similar study, wolves raised by people didn't produce the same effect, which suggests the loop developed as dogs were domesticated by humans.

Lower odds of dementia in older adults

A 2023 study from the Tokyo Metropolitan Institute for Geriatrics and Gerontology followed 11,194 adults aged 65 to 84 for about four years. Current dog owners had 40% lower odds of developing disabling dementia than people who had never owned or no longer owned a dog. Cat ownership showed no meaningful effect. The benefit was strongest for dog owners who exercised regularly and weren't socially isolated. Researcher Yu Taniguchi said dog care "helps people maintain daily exercise habits and opportunities for social participation."

Possible protection against childhood asthma

A 2015 study in JAMA Pediatrics examined more than one million Swedish children. Kids who lived with a dog during their first year had a 13% lower risk of asthma at age 6. Lead author Tove Fall of Uppsala University told Reuters the findings suggest expectant parents may not need to give away the family pet for fear their baby will develop asthma. A broad 2022 review of child-dog research also linked growing up with dogs to more physical activity, less stress and greater empathy.

The important caveats

Nearly all this research is observational. It shows that dog owners tend to be healthier, not that dogs cause better health. People who choose to get a dog may already be more active, healthier or better off financially. The 2019 meta-analysis noted its pooled results weren't adjusted for those factors, and the Swedish stroke study couldn't account for smoking.

Some findings are mixed. A 2024 Australian study found dog owners were more active, but only exercise done apart from the dog was tied to less loneliness and better mental health. Its authors warned against telling people to get a dog purely for their mental health. Allergy research is mixed as well, with some studies finding higher rates of pet allergy and asthma among children exposed to dogs.

The bottom line

No study proves that a dog will add years to your life. Still, the pattern is consistent across countries, age groups and millions of people: dog owners tend to move more, recover better from serious heart problems and stay sharper as they age. For many, the daily walks, routine and companionship of a dog may be one of the more enjoyable health habits there is in life.
