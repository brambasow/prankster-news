Your Pay Compared to the CEO's of Top US Companies

The typical S&P 500 chief executive made $17.7 million in 2025, about 200 times what the median worker at the same companies earned. Here is how CEO pay breaks down by industry and age, and what keeps pushing the gap wider.

7:45 PM EST November 21, 2026 · 12 min read · By Bram Basow

The typical pay package for a chief executive in the S&P 500 grew nearly 6% in 2025, to $17.7 million, according to the annual Associated Press survey of Equilar data. The median employee at those same companies made $89,744, up 4.7%. At half the companies in the survey, a typical worker would need about 200 years on the job to earn what the boss collected in one.

That ratio has crept up every year lately. Equilar put it at 192 to 1 for 2024 and 200 to 1 for 2025. Measured other ways, the gap looks even wider, and the reasons behind it go back decades, to how boards pay executives, how big American companies have become, and how much bargaining power ordinary workers have lost.

Three ways to measure the top

There is no single official number for CEO pay. The figure you see depends on who is counting, which companies they include, and whether they use the value of stock when it is promised or when it is cashed in.

The last row is a reminder that most people with "CEO" on their business card are not millionaires. The federal wage survey counts everyone who runs an organization, from a regional hospital to a family manufacturer, and it only counts wages. Big-company bosses are a tiny group at the very top of that pyramid. The median S&P 500 package is roughly 66 times the average chief executive wage in the BLS data.

Outliers make averages hard to use. Tesla reported a grant-date value of about $158.3 billion for the stock awards it gave Elon Musk in 2025, a sum the AFL-CIO calculated was 14 times larger than the pay of every other S&P 500 chief executive combined. Part of that grant was later forfeited, and most of the rest pays out only if Tesla hits very aggressive targets. Welltower's Shankh Mitra was credited with $821.1 million, an award meant to cover roughly a decade. "Grant-date fair value is an accounting estimate, not the amount an executive ultimately receives," The Conference Board cautioned in its 2026 review.

How the gap grew

The Economic Policy Institute has tracked the pay of top CEOs against typical workers since 1965. Back then, the head of a big company made about 21 times what a typical worker earned. By 2000, at the peak of the dot-com stock boom, the multiple was close to 380. It fell after crashes in 2001 and 2008, then climbed again with the market. EPI estimates realized CEO pay rose 14% in 2025, to $27.9 million, while pay for typical workers in those companies' industries rose 2.4% after inflation.

Over the whole stretch from 1978 to 2025, EPI finds top CEO pay grew 1,316% after inflation, while a typical worker's pay grew 28%.

CEO pay by industry

Two very different data sets answer the question "what does a CEO make in my industry?" They should not be mixed. The first comes from proxy statements filed by the largest public companies and includes stock awards. The second is the government's wage survey, which covers chief executives at organizations of every size but leaves out stock and most bonuses.

The table shows that the size of the pay ratio often says more about the workforce than about the CEO. Utilities and oil and gas producers report median worker pay of about $150,000, so their ratios stay modest even with eight-figure CEO packages. Retailers, restaurant chains and entertainment companies have large part-time and seasonal staffs, and the SEC's disclosure rule counts those workers at their actual pay when companies identify their median employee. Coca-Cola's median worker, for example, earned $17,947 and its ratio was 1,739 to 1, while TJX reported a median employee pay of $14,994 against its CEO's $26.6 million, a ratio of 1,774 to 1.

The Conference Board, using a broader set of 3,000 public companies, found that sector matters less than size. Median CEO pay in that group ranged from $3.4 million at companies with under $100 million in revenue to $23.6 million at those with $50 billion or more. Communication services had the highest sector median, $8.9 million.

CEO pay by age: what the data can and can't tell you

There is no reliable public data on how CEO pay changes with age. The government's wage survey doesn't record ages at all, and none of the big executive pay studies we reviewed breaks pay out by age group. Salary websites publish pay-by-experience estimates, but they are built from self-reported entries that we could not check against a second source, so we have not used them.

What the data does show is who holds the job. The Census Bureau's monthly household survey, run for the BLS, counted about 1.8 million people who described their job as chief executive in 2025. That group is broader than the payroll count above because it includes owners and people at very small firms. Their median age was 51.6, almost a decade above the 42.1 median for all workers.

At the largest companies the bosses are older. The Conference Board found the average S&P 500 chief executive was about 58 in 2025, and Diligent Market Intelligence put the figure at 59. Equilar's CEO tracker found that executives who left the top job at large companies in 2025 averaged 62, while their replacements averaged 55; Spencer Stuart put departing S&P 500 CEOs at 62.9. Spencer Stuart reported that new CEOs across the S&P 1500 averaged 54.4 in 2025, down from 55.8 a year earlier. Because pay rises with company size and the biggest companies tend to hire experienced executives in their 50s, age and pay are tangled together, but no public data set separates the two.

What's driving the gap

Economists broadly agree on the forces at work. They disagree, often sharply, about whether those forces reflect a healthy market for scarce talent or executives who have learned to set their own pay.

1. Pay comes mostly in stock, and stocks have soared. Stock awards are now the largest piece of a typical package. Equilar found the median S&P 500 stock award rose 11.5% in 2025, to $10.9 million, while salaries barely moved. The Conference Board reports that since 2021, median performance-based stock awards at S&P 500 companies are up 46%, compared with about 16% for base salaries. When the market rises, those shares are worth more by the time they vest. EPI's figures show realized CEO pay rising 14% in 2025, the same year EPI's inflation-adjusted measure of the S&P 500 rose 11.8%. A worker paid only in wages gets none of that lift.

2. Peer benchmarking and the "Lake Wobegon" effect. Boards set pay by comparing their CEO with a hand-picked group of peer companies, and few boards want to admit their leader is below average. Economists Rachel Hayes and Scott Schaefer examined the pattern in a 2009 paper titled "CEO Pay and the Lake Wobegon Effect," a nod to Garrison Keillor's fictional town where "all the children are above average." Research by John Bizjak, Michael Lemmon and others found that benchmarking lifts pay for CEOs who fall below their peer median, and that peer groups tend to include larger, better-paying firms. Each raise then becomes part of the next company's benchmark.

3. Bigger companies, superstar economics. Economists Xavier Gabaix and Augustin Landier argued in a 2008 paper that CEO pay tracks the size of the companies CEOs run. Their model found the sixfold rise in CEO pay from 1980 to 2003 matched a sixfold rise in the market value of large U.S. companies. If a slightly better CEO can add billions to a giant firm's value, the reasoning goes, boards will pay heavily to get one. The Conference Board's size data, with a nearly sevenfold gap between the smallest and largest companies, fits that pattern.

4. Boards and compensation consultants. Law professors Lucian Bebchuk and Jesse Fried, both now at Harvard, argued in their book "Pay Without Performance" that CEOs often have enough influence over the directors who set their pay to push it above what a true arm's-length deal would produce. They describe consultants who depend on management for repeat business and pay designs that make large sums less visible. Since 2012, SEC rules adopted under the Dodd-Frank Act have required compensation committees to weigh a consultant's independence before hiring one, though they are not barred from hiring advisers with conflicts. EPI's Elise Gould and Josh Bivens put the case bluntly, writing that CEO pay has climbed because executives "gained and used increasing leverage over the corporate boards that set their pay."

5. Say-on-pay has little bite. Since 2011, shareholders at public companies have had a nonbinding vote on executive pay, held at least once every three years. In practice, nearly every plan passes. The Conference Board found that in 2026, 76% of pay plans at Russell 3000 companies won at least 90% support, and only 0.9% failed. At S&P 500 companies, 1.1% failed. Even a failed vote does not force a board to change anything.

6. Stock buybacks. S&P 500 companies spent a record $1.02 trillion buying back their own shares in the 12 months through September 2025, according to S&P Dow Jones Indices. Buybacks reduce the number of shares, which raises earnings per share and can support the stock price, both common targets in executive pay plans. In a 2018 study of 385 buybacks, then-SEC Commissioner Robert Jackson found that at least one executive sold shares within a month of about half of the announcements. Critics, including economist William Lazonick, argue that buybacks steer cash to shareholders and executives that could have gone to wages or investment. Companies say buybacks return spare cash to owners when they lack better uses for it.

7. Weaker unions and slow-growing wages. The other half of the ratio is the worker. Union membership fell from 20.1% of wage and salary workers in 1983 to 10.0% in 2025, according to the BLS. Labor economists, especially at EPI, link that decline to slower wage growth for typical workers, who have less leverage to claim a share of rising profits. Median weekly earnings for full-time workers were $1,232 in the third quarter of 2026, up 1.5% from a year earlier, the BLS reported. That works out to about $64,000 a year.

8. The tax code. In 1993, Congress capped the corporate tax deduction for executive pay at $1 million a year under Section 162(m) of the tax code, but exempted pay linked to performance. Studies, including work by economists Tod Perry and Marc Zenner, found companies held salaries near $1 million and shifted pay toward stock options and incentive awards that qualified for the exemption. The 2017 tax law removed the performance exemption, and the 2021 American Rescue Plan will extend the $1 million cap to a company's next five highest-paid employees starting with tax years that begin after December 31, 2026. Pay kept rising after the 2017 change: the Conference Board's median S&P 500 figure went from $10.7 million in 2017 filings to $17.5 million in 2026. The 2021 expansion has not taken effect yet.

The case for high CEO pay

Defenders of current pay levels argue that the market for top talent sets the price. Economists Steven Kaplan of the University of Chicago and Joshua Rauh of Stanford found that pay for other top earners, such as hedge fund managers, top lawyers, and executives at privately held companies that face no shareholder votes, rose about as fast as public-company CEO pay. In their view, that points to technology and scale rewarding talent across the economy rather than boards being captured. Gabaix and Landier's size model makes a similar case.

Supporters also note that much of a modern package only pays off if the stock or other targets perform, and that reported figures can overstate what executives actually take home. The Conference Board points out that both Tesla's and Welltower's headline numbers reflect multiyear or contingent awards. Critics answer that stock-based pay rewards CEOs for broad market rallies they did not create, and that the Kaplan comparison leaves out how far top pay has pulled away from everyone else. By EPI's count, the CEOs in its sample earned 2.3 times the average of the top 0.1% of wage earners in 1965 and about nine times as much by 2000.

What could change

Some cities are trying to tax the gap directly. Since 2020, San Francisco has charged an extra business tax on companies whose top executive earns more than 100 times the median pay of their local employees. In Los Angeles, unions began collecting signatures in early 2026 for a similar tax on large employers whose top executives earn more than 50 times what their typical local worker does. EPI has proposed requiring collective bargaining by default at companies with especially lopsided ratios. The Howard Jarvis Taxpayers Association has objected that the Los Angeles proposal is written to avoid the two-thirds vote normally required for special taxes. Next year brings the wider federal deduction cap, and pay ratio disclosures for 2026 will start appearing in proxy statements in the spring.
