Trump, Xi and Putin to Meet in Shenzhen in First-Ever Three-Way Summit

The leaders of the world's three biggest nuclear powers plan to sit down together for the first time.

9:32 PM EST November 14, 2026 · 4 min read · by Prankster News Staff

The leaders of the world's three biggest nuclear powers plan to sit down together for the first time. U.S. President Donald Trump, Chinese President Xi Jinping and Russian President Vladimir Putin will hold a trilateral meeting on Thursday, Nov. 19, on the sidelines of the Asia-Pacific Economic Cooperation (APEC) summit in Shenzhen, China, according to the White House and the Kremlin.

Kremlin spokesman Dmitry Peskov confirmed the date on Saturday. "An agreement has been reached to hold a trilateral meeting on the 19th between President Putin, Chairman Xi and President Trump," he told the Russian state news agency Tass. Beijing has been quieter. As of Friday, Chinese state media had reported only that Xi would hold "bilateral" talks at the summit, according to the BBC, and hadn't mentioned the three-way meeting.

The idea isn't new. Bloomberg reported in September that Putin and Xi had discussed possible trilateral talks with Trump at APEC, according to Kremlin aide Yuri Ushakov. Trump arrives late Wednesday for the two-day summit of 21 member economies. That follows an ASEAN summit in the Philippines on Tuesday, and he'll also hold a bilateral meeting with Xi on Thursday, their fourth since Trump returned to office.

Nuclear arms at the top of the agenda

The White House says nuclear weapons will lead the discussion. "The president has made it clear that, among other topics, he will discuss the reduction of nuclear weapons in the world, whose proliferation President Trump believes is very dangerous," White House spokesperson Olivia Wales told reporters on Friday.

That matters because New START, the last treaty capping U.S. and Russian strategic arsenals, expired in February. That left the two largest nuclear arsenals without limits for the first time in more than half a century. Trump turned down Russia's offer of a one-year extension and instead demanded a "new, improved and modernized" deal that includes China. The UN has urged both sides to agree on a new framework quickly.

China is the sticking point. Russia has the most warheads, followed by the U.S. and then China, according to the Stockholm International Peace Research Institute. In February, a Chinese spokesman said Beijing "will not participate in nuclear disarmament negotiations at this stage." Tsinghua University scholar Sun Chenghao, quoted by the Lowy Institute, warned that placing China in the same framework "risks turning unequal capabilities into nominally equal responsibilities." Bloomberg likewise called denuclearization "an unlikely prospect" given China's reluctance.

A senior U.S. official called Thursday's session an "entry point" and "the first conversation," saying it's "time for China to sort of step up… to the adults' table." Trump also plans to raise the war with Iran. Ukraine is officially off the trilateral agenda: U.S. officials say Ukraine talks are a separate track China isn't part of, although Kremlin officials have suggested the war could come up. No separate Trump-Putin meeting is scheduled.

Low expectations, high symbolism

Nobody involved expects a breakthrough. U.S. officials say no agreement is likely, and Reuters reports the format and agenda were still being negotiated days before the meeting. The Center for Strategic and International Studies (CSIS) says any nuclear outcome will probably be "more of a political statement than a verifiable and restrictive arms control agreement." It argues the U.S. should use the talks to lay groundwork for a longer-term trilateral agenda. Even so, the meeting matters to each side.

  • For arms control, it's the first time all three major nuclear powers' leaders will discuss limits face to face, at a moment when no treaty caps the U.S. and Russian arsenals.
  • For Putin, analysts say it offers a "first among equals" photo opportunity and a way to project influence while the Ukraine war continues, now nearly five years old. Reuters reports he sees a chance to push for a Ukraine settlement on his terms.
  • For Xi, hosting gives China center stage, but Reuters and the Lowy Institute describe Beijing as wary. Xi's scripted style clashes with Trump's improvisation, and China wants to avoid being pressured into arms cuts or looking like it's carving up the world with Moscow and Washington.
  • For Trump, it's a chance at a historic diplomatic moment following his party's losses in the recent midterms, with a U.S.-China trade truce already extended until January 2027.

What allies will be watching

The image of three leaders deciding global security questions without Europe, Ukraine or Asian allies in the room has already earned the meeting the nickname "Yalta 2.0," after the 1945 conference where the Allied powers shaped postwar Europe. CSIS notes that even talking about arms control in this format may make U.S. allies nervous. Even if Thursday produces only a handshake and a promise to keep talking, how Washington, Beijing and Moscow describe the session afterward will shape views of arms control, the Iran war and the balance of power.
