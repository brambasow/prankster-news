Digital Price Tags: Efficiency Tool or Path to Personalized Pricing?

Walmart, Kroger, and other retailers are swapping paper shelf tags for digital price screens.

3:24 PM EST November 4, 2026 · 4 min read · by Prankster News Staff

Retailers including Walmart, Kroger, Best Buy, and others are replacing traditional paper shelf tags with electronic shelf labels (ESLs), small battery-powered digital screens that display prices and product information. Walmart has been rolling these out widely, with the technology already in thousands of U.S. stores and plans for broader coverage.

The labels connect to store systems so prices can be updated centrally and quickly, rather than requiring workers to manually swap paper tags. Retailers say this improves accuracy (reducing mismatches between shelves and checkout), speeds up markdowns on perishable or slow-moving items, helps with inventory management, and frees staff for customer service. Some systems also include features like lights or QR codes that help shoppers or employees locate items.

Why the Concerns?

Consumer advocates, unions, and some lawmakers worry that the same technology that makes price changes easier could enable less consumer-friendly practices:

  • Dynamic or surge pricing: Prices could rise quickly in response to demand, weather, time of day, or local conditions (for example, ice cream during a heat wave). Critics note that airlines and rideshare apps already use similar approaches.
  • Personalized or “surveillance” pricing: In theory, stores could combine ESL systems with customer data (loyalty apps, purchase history, device signals, or even cameras) to show different prices to different shoppers for the same item. Advocacy groups such as Groundwork Collaborative have highlighted a Walmart patent (granted in 2023) describing the ability to adjust prices dynamically based on items already in a shopper’s cart or other factors. They argue companies do not invest in such technology without intending to use it to increase profits.
  • Labor impacts: Unions, including the AFL-CIO, have warned that widespread ESL adoption could eliminate tens of thousands of jobs tied to manual price changes and reduce wages.
  • Transparency and trust: With paper tags, shoppers can sometimes see employees updating prices. Digital systems feel more opaque. Surveys have shown many consumers distrust retailers on pricing and would trust them even less if prices varied based on personal data.

Several states have introduced or passed measures restricting ESLs or “surveillance pricing.” Federal bills such as the Stop Price Gouging in Grocery Stores Act have been proposed. The FTC has also signaled scrutiny of personalized pricing practices.

Retailers’ Response

Walmart CEO John Furner has publicly addressed the concerns in letters, stating the company “prices the product, not the person.” He has pledged that Walmart does not and will not set different prices based on a customer’s income, shopping history, urgency, perceived willingness to pay, or the time of day. Executives have said the labels themselves do not collect personal information, lack cameras or microphones, and operate primarily as display tools. Walmart has also said it will not use or renew the contested patent.

The Broader Picture

The technology itself is not new; European and some U.S. retailers have used versions for years. What has changed is the scale of adoption in major U.S. chains combined with advances in AI, data analytics, and online personalized pricing. Whether ESLs remain primarily an efficiency tool or evolve into something more dynamic will depend on company policies, competitive pressure, consumer pushback, and regulation.

For now, shoppers are advised to check prices carefully at checkout, compare store and online prices, and watch for any discrepancies. Retailers have an incentive to maintain trust with low-price or value-focused brands, but the capability for rapid, data-driven changes exists once the digital infrastructure is in place. The debate reflects a larger tension between operational efficiency and consumer protection in an increasingly data-rich environment.
