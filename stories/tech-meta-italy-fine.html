Meta in the Spotlight as Italy Fines €805 Million for Accessing Users' Photos Without Consent

Italy's data protection authority has fined Meta €805 million (about $887 million) after finding that the Facebook app for Android accessed photos, videos and their metadata on Italian users' phones without specific consent.

11:04 PM EST November 14, 2026 · 3 min read · by Prankster News Staff

Italy's data protection authority has fined Meta €805 million (about $887 million) after finding that the Facebook app for Android accessed photos, videos and their metadata on Italian users' phones without specific consent. Politico reports it is by far the largest fine the Italian regulator has ever imposed.

The authority, known as the Garante, announced the decision Saturday in Rome. It said Meta Platforms Ireland Limited gained access to the material "without obtaining specific consent and without properly informing" users. About 20 million Android users in Italy were affected.

The feature at the center of the case

The ruling concerns a Facebook feature called "Get tips from your gallery while browsing Facebook," available since 2023. As people scroll, it suggests photos and videos from their phone's camera roll as ideas for new posts.

The investigation began in July 2025 after a complaint from a consumer protection group in Bolzano, and the regulator ran its own technical analysis. It found three problems:

  1. Gallery access was requested through a single permission covering the whole app and several features with different needs.
  2. The permission request came after a vague notice to "enable other features," so users couldn't tell they were also switching on gallery suggestions.
  3. The feature was turned on by default.

"If granted in full, this type of permission allows the app to read the device's entire photo archive, usually permanently, without the user or the authorities being able to verify which information is actually being used," the Garante said.

What Meta must change

Meta has 60 days to bring the feature into line. It must ask for consent through a separate, specific request and clearly explain what the feature does, that it involves gallery access, and how to turn it off. The feature must stay off by default, including for users who have already granted permission. If Meta misses the deadline, the feature can't run for anyone who hasn't given valid consent. The company must also report its first steps within 30 days and the measures it has taken within 90 days.

How the fine was set

The regulator said it weighed the large number of people affected and the length of the violation. It says the violation is still ongoing for users who haven't updated the app and for those who already granted the system permission. It also counted a previous 2019 decision against the company and what it called Meta's poor cooperation during the investigation. Changes Meta made after the complaint counted in its favor, but the Garante called them late and limited to users who update the app. The fine equals about 0.47% of Meta's global revenue.

Meta says it will appeal

Meta strongly disputes the decision. "We strongly disagree with the Italian Data Protection Authority's assessment of the facts and its application of the law, and we believe the fine is disproportionate," a spokesperson said. "This is a simple feature that helps people share photos and videos on Facebook. Users must grant permission on their device before the feature can operate, and they can change that permission at any time in their settings." The company said it intends to appeal.

Why it matters

The case is notable for which law it rests on. Most big EU privacy fines against Meta come from Ireland's regulator under the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR), because Meta's European headquarters is in Dublin. Here, Italy relied on its own law implementing the EU's ePrivacy directive, which treats a smartphone as part of a person's private sphere. Under that law, any access to what's stored on a phone requires free and informed consent, regardless of if the data is transmitted or anyone is harmed. Meta argued the case belonged with the Irish authority, but the Garante rejected that, saying the ePrivacy rules have no "one-stop-shop" mechanism. If that reasoning holds on appeal, other national regulators could pursue similar cases against Facebook and its parent company Meta.

The investigation didn't cover whether gallery content was used to train artificial intelligence models, which Meta denies doing. The Garante said it reserves the right to examine separately a newer feature, launched in 2026, that lets gallery content be processed in the cloud. That sets up a possible next round of scrutiny as tech companies push AI tools that reach deeper into users' personal files.
