The Supreme Court Needs Term Limits, and Democrats Finally Have a Real Opening

About eight in ten Americans want fixed terms for the justices. Democrats will soon hold at least 258 House seats and 56 Senate seats, enough to pass an 18-year plan in the House and force the issue in the Senate, though the filibuster and a likely veto still stand in the way.

2:37 AM EST November 24, 2026 · 8 min read · by Prankster News Staff

When the new Congress is sworn in this January, Democrats will hold at least 258 House seats, their largest majority since 1992, and 56 of the 100 Senate seats. They should spend part of that power on a reform that voters in both parties already back: ending lifetime tenure on the Supreme Court and giving each justice one fixed, 18-year term.

Life tenure has outgrown its purpose

The Constitution says federal judges keep their posts "during good behaviour," a rule meant to shield them from political pressure. For most of the country's history, that worked out to roughly 15 years on the high court. For justices who have left the bench since 1970, the average is about 26 years, according to the Brennan Center, close to the gap the conservative law professors Steven Calabresi and James Lindgren documented when they made their own case for term limits two decades ago. Justice Clarence Thomas, seated in 1991, became the second-longest-serving justice ever this May and is on pace to pass William O. Douglas's record in 2028.

Longer stays have turned the Court's makeup into a matter of luck and timing. Jimmy Carter never filled a single seat, while Donald Trump filled three in his first term. Justices try to retire when a like-minded president can name their successor, and both parties now hunt for young nominees who could serve for 30 years or more. In 2016, Senate Republicans refused to act on Merrick Garland's nomination for the rest of the year. In 2020, they confirmed Amy Coney Barrett days before an election in which millions had already voted.

The states gave up on this model long ago. In 49 states, high court judges face fixed terms, a mandatory retirement age or both. Rhode Island is the lone exception, and state justices since 1970 have served about 13 years on average, by the Brennan Center count. An 18-year term, with a new justice appointed in the first and third years of every presidential term, would give each president two picks, let the Court shift gradually with the voters, and lower the stakes of any single confirmation fight. Even John Roberts, as a young White House lawyer in 1983, wrote that there was "much to be said" for swapping life tenure for a set term of years.

The public made up its mind a while ago

The most recent Marquette Law School national poll, taken Sept. 2 to 9, found 79% of adults favor fixed terms for the justices and 21% oppose them. Support came from 88% of Democrats, 75% of independents and 71% of Republicans. Marquette's May survey had the same 79%, up from 71% in 2019, with a high of 83% in October 2024.

Other polls point the same way. In a Navigator Research survey of registered voters taken May 13 to 18, 74% backed term limits, and supporters outnumbered opponents even among Republicans who call themselves MAGA. When a Strength In Numbers/Verasight poll in February asked specifically about an 18-year limit, 65% said yes, 15% said no and 20% weren't sure. The trend isn't new: an AP-NORC poll in July 2022 found 67% support, including 57% of Republicans. By comparison, adding seats to the Court splits the country, with Marquette putting it at 53% in favor and just 35% among Republicans. Term limits are the reform with a genuine bipartisan majority.

The plans already on the table

Democrats would not be starting from scratch. Rep. Hank Johnson of Georgia reintroduced the Supreme Court TERM Act (H.R. 3544) in May 2025. It gives justices 18 years of regular active service before they move to senior status, schedules appointments in the first and third years after each presidential election, and moves current justices to senior status by seniority as new ones are confirmed. In July, Sen. Sheldon Whitehouse of Rhode Island introduced the Supreme Court Biennial Appointments and Term Limits Act (S. 5076), which also guarantees two appointments per presidential term and limits appeals to the nine most recently appointed justices. Both bills were sent to their chambers' Judiciary committees and have gone nowhere under Republican control.

They build on earlier work. President Joe Biden's July 2024 plan called for the same 18-year, two-year rhythm, a binding ethics code for the justices, and a constitutional amendment, which he called No One Is Above the Law, to strip presidents of broad criminal immunity. None of it passed. The bipartisan commission Biden created in 2021 laid out the arguments on each side without recommending term limits, and its members split on whether Congress could create them without an amendment.

The legal fight: statute or amendment?

That split is the hardest question. The Brennan Center's Alicia Bannon and Michael Milov-Cordoba, along with law professors Paul Carrington and Roger Cramton before them, argue that a statute works. In their view, "good behaviour" protects a judge's place in the federal judiciary and their pay, not a permanent seat on the Supreme Court. Congress already sets the Court's size and much of its jurisdiction, and it created senior status, and a 1934 Supreme Court decision held that Congress may lighten judges' duties without removing them from office.

Skeptics say only an amendment will do. Calabresi and Lindgren concluded that pushing justices off the Court by statute would collide with the Good Behaviour Clause. Commission members on that side added that the Appointments Clause treats "judges of the supreme Court" as an office of its own. A Congressional Research Service analysis has said most commentators agree an amendment would be needed. Erwin Chemerinsky, dean of Berkeley Law and a term-limits supporter, has said the same, especially if the limits reach sitting justices. Whatever Congress passes, the justices would get the final word on whether it is constitutional. That argues for a statute that applies only to future justices and is written to survive that review.

How Democrats could actually get it done

The House. This is the easy part. A simple majority passes a bill, and Hakeem Jeffries, expected to be the next Speaker, wrote that Democrats must "clean up the mess at the Supreme Court," and has said Rep. Jamie Raskin, the likely Judiciary Committee chair, would take the lead. A term-limits bill could clear the House in 2027.

The Senate filibuster. Most bills need 60 votes to end debate. With 56 seats, Democrats would need four Republicans, or more if any Democrat defects. The Senate has changed this rule by simple majority before: Democrats ended filibusters on most nominations in 2013, and Republicans extended that to Supreme Court nominees in 2017. A narrow carve-out for court reform would follow the same playbook. Chuck Schumer has supported carve-outs for voting rights and abortion rights, but the 2022 attempt for voting rights failed 48 to 52 when two Democrats voted no. This time, Democrats could lose five of their own and still reach 51. Senators such as Jeff Merkley and Tim Kaine are already pushing to bring back the talking filibuster, which would force opponents to hold the floor.

The veto. President Trump has said he will veto "a lot of things" a Democratic Congress sends him. We found no public statement from him on term limits specifically. Overriding a veto takes two-thirds of both chambers, 290 House votes and 67 in the Senate, so Democrats would need about 32 House Republicans and 11 GOP senators. That is a long shot. The likeliest path is a bill that exempts the current justices and starts after Trump's term ends, which takes away the argument that it is aimed at anyone sitting on the bench now. If he vetoes it anyway, Democrats will have put every member of Congress on record ahead of 2028.

Budget reconciliation. This shortcut won't work. Reconciliation avoids the filibuster only for tax and spending changes, and the Senate's Byrd rule strikes any provision whose budget effect is merely incidental to its policy purpose. Changing how justices serve is a policy change, so it would almost certainly be thrown out.

A constitutional amendment, and the states. An amendment would end the legal debate, but it needs two-thirds of both chambers and ratification by 38 states. Democrats are short in both chambers. States cannot set the terms of federal judges themselves. Their role is to ratify an amendment, or, if two-thirds of them apply, call a convention to propose one, a route that has never been used. A statute now, with an amendment as the long-term fix, is the realistic order.

The bottom line

Life tenure made sense when justices served about 15 years. It makes little sense when one justice has already served under six presidents and could keep going for years. The public has been clear for years, the bills are written, and Democrats are about to control both chambers by their widest margins since Barack Obama's first term. They may not get a law signed before 2029. But a vote in the House, a serious fight over the Senate rules, and a clear record for voters to judge in 2028 would be the most progress this idea has ever made.
